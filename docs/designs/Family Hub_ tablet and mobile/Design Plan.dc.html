01

What I took from the paper

The two documents already have a design point of view and it is a good one. I am not starting from scratch; I am translating a page meant to be written on into a page meant to be read across a room. Three things carry over intact, one thing has to change.

Carries over
Letterspaced micro-caps

Every label on both documents is small, uppercase and widely tracked. It is how the paper stays quiet while still being organised. It becomes the Hub's only label style.

Carries over
Money is set in serif

$6,618.78 and $4,650 are the loudest things on the paper and they are serif, not sans. Figures stay serif everywhere in the Hub. It is why the product will not read as a budgeting app.

Carries over
One warm accent, earned

The register uses brass on precisely five things: the pull-quote, the action queue, the two switched policies, the overleaf line. Nothing decorative is coloured. That discipline is the whole reason a wall of amber never happens.

Has to change
The grid of columns

Eight columns work at arm's length with a pen. At a metre they collapse. Rows become two-line stacks: the fact on top, its qualifiers beneath. Same information, half the width, four times the size.

Three things in the paper that differ from the brief
  • The current card is September: $4,650 target, $1,163 per week — the brief says August's $4,510 / $1,128. I will design against September, and against Week 4 being 9 days with a pro-rata target of $1,495, because a scorecard that cannot express a short week is broken in the first month I ship it.
  • The mortgage is mid-switch to Peoples Choice at 5.84%, saving ~$2,231/yr. That is a second savings win in flight and roughly one and a half times the home insurance one. Savings Won needs to hold a pending row, not just a done one.
  • The register has rows with no cost at all — Mazda CTP "Insurer TBC", boat licence "TBC". Missing cost is as normal as missing renewal date, and both are small calls to action rather than gaps.

Palette

Twelve values, three of which carry meaning and nine of which are structure. The meaning is the important part: ink states a fact, green marks progress, brass asks for something. No fourth semantic colour will be added — severity is expressed in type size, never in hue, which is how "two states, not a gradient" survives contact with nine modules.

Typefaces and their jobs

Two families, split by job rather than by hierarchy: the serif holds anything true about the world — money, dates, names, the next action — and the sans holds anything the interface is saying about it. Once that rule is in place, a number never needs a label to look like a number.

04

Layout concept — the Page and the Slip

One idea, and it comes from the fridge itself: the iPad is a page pinned to the door, and a phone is a slip of paper you fill in and hand over. The page never scrolls if I can help it, has no navigation chrome, and is always already open. The slip is a single-purpose form with a large target for every finger.

Whatever the house needs right now, at the largest size on the screen. Time of day decides what lives here — the uniform answer before school, the spending number on Sunday evening, the stalled list on Saturday morning.

How the fridge shows read-only without looking disabled

Your §13 question, and it is answered by removal plus one addition. Removal: no buttons, no chevrons, no rounded rectangles anywhere on the wall, no tap targets except the avatar strip, the week letter and a chore tick. Nothing on the page looks pressable, so nothing looks broken for not being pressable.

The addition: the three things that are tappable share one visual signature that appears nowhere else — a solid ink underline, 3px, sitting directly under the text. Learn it once, and the whole wall's interaction model is known. Everything else is print.

05

Working principles

Your seven product principles restated as things I can be held to at the pixel level. Each one has a consequence I have to live with.

1
Severity is typographic, never chromatic

A job stalled 142 days and one stalled 24 days are the same brass, different type sizes. Consequence: the stalled list is a size ramp, and the worst job is physically the biggest thing on the screen.

2
Every generated claim carries its source and its age, inline

Not in a settings pane, not on hover — a mist-coloured micro-caps line beneath the claim: School email · 6:04 am. Consequence: entered content and generated content are visibly different species, and the family learns to read freshness without ever being told.

3
Stale is a state of the ink, not a banner

A value that failed to refresh drops from Ink to Mist and keeps its timestamp. It is still the last known truth and still readable. Consequence: no spinners, no skeletons, no error dialogs anywhere in the product.

4
A cost never appears without its basis

$346.50 per month and ~$175 working avg / mo are different kinds of number. Actuals get full ink and no marker; working averages and estimates keep the tilde and set the basis line in italic serif. Consequence: nothing in the register is ever rounded into a lie, and the same rule governs asset estimates — "2026 estimate" is a basis, not a footnote.

5
Objects decay. People do not.

Days-since, stall lists and dated attention states apply to jobs, assets, renewals and slots. Goals and chores get next step and a week that resets clean. Consequence: no counter, streak, bar or brass mark ever appears next to a child's name — and I will treat that as a bug, not a preference.

6
Debt and progress are always set as a pair

Never a number owed without the matching number won, at matching weight. $2,462/mo being reduced sits beside $1,449 saved; seven stalled jobs sit beside the count moved this month. Consequence: no screen in the product can be read as purely bad news, which is the difference between a wall that gets looked at and one that gets ignored.

7
The house speaks in its own sentences

Empty states are invitations: "Nothing has moved in a fortnight. Pick one." Not "No items." The register's own lines — "Nothing renews without a conscious decision" — are set in italic serif and used as running heads, the way the paper uses them. Consequence: zero task-management vocabulary. No backlog, no items, no cards, no tasks, no dashboard-speak.

8
A correction always reads as chosen

Hand-corrected content — a week letter someone flipped, a class a parent fixed after re-import — is the most trustworthy content in the system, and gets full ink plus a small attribution: Set by Matt · Mon. Consequence: nothing that a human overruled ever renders in an error or warning treatment. Your timetable is corrected in four places in blue pen and those four are the right ones.

06

The three kinds of money

Deliverable 12.9, resolved as a typographic scheme rather than a legend. Three numbers, three different sentence shapes, and the shape tells you what kind of money it is before you have read the figure.

Recurring · the register
$2,462/mo
and falling

Set with a rate. It has a direction, so it always carries the savings-won figure and the verb. Green, because the story is progress.

Capital · the assets
$318/mo
to not be surprised

Set with a horizon, never a target. Illustrative figure — it comes out of the real asset list. Unpainted ink: it is neither a debt nor a win, just arithmetic about a 20-year-old house.

Discretionary · the scorecard
$1,163/wk
of $4,650 this month

Set with a pace. The only one of the three with a week in it, and the only one that can be over. Weekly on the wall; the month total is a phone number.

The unit is the tell: a rate that is falling, a rate with a horizon, a pace against a target. Nobody needs to be taught that. And the scorecard's own "Not on this card" list travels with the $4,650 wherever it appears, stated flatly and without apology — it is the sentence that stops $4,650 reading as what the family costs to run.

08

Growing from one module to nine

The Page absorbs modules by getting denser at the bottom and never by adding chrome. Nothing re-lays-out when a module arrives, because the standing head, the answer zone and the row stack exist from day one — the stack just gets longer and the rows get shorter.

State A · now
The stalled list is the home screen

No dashboard, no tiles, no avatar strip — there is nothing personal behind it yet, and four discs that lead nowhere would be the first lie the product tells. The standing head and the answer zone are already there, holding the worst job.

State B · three modules
The list steps down, the stack appears

Projects gives up the answer zone and becomes the first row in the stack. A module arriving is a row appearing at its rank in the decay order, which is a small, legible change — not a re-shuffle.

State C · all nine
Nine modules, five rows

The wall stays glanceable only if calm modules are allowed to be absent. A module with nothing to say prints no row — it lives behind the avatar strip or one tap down. Nine modules do not mean nine rows, and if the wall ever needs a tenth row, the answer is a shorter wall.

09

Where the joy goes

Your note. The rule I would work to: the surprises are dry, they are rare, and they are all about this house specifically. Nothing congratulates anyone. A widget that says "Great job!" is exactly the thing Rose will use to decide the whole app is embarrassing. Six candidates, and I would ship two or three.

The strike-through

When a job finally moves, 142 days does not vanish — it gets a line drawn through it and sits there for the rest of the day before the counter resets. You beat it, and you get to see the number you beat.

Week closed

The eleventh Sunday number lands and the card takes a letterpress stamp — WEEK CLOSED, slightly off-square, like it was done by hand. The whole ritual gets a full stop.

The house line

One line in the footer that is about the house and not about the family's obligations: water 22°, high tide 4:12, the moon, the first frost that will never come. It changes daily and asks for nothing. This is the reason to look when nothing needs you.

Anniversaries of wins

One year on from the insurance switch, the register mentions it once: "a year ago today. $1,449, still working." The family's own scoreboard, keeping its own score.

Rare empty states

Nothing stalled is a genuinely unusual event and the screen is allowed to say so: "Nothing has stalled. This has not happened before." Dry, factual, and quietly a bit funny because it is true.

The tick

The one tactile thing in the product, performed by an 11-year-old with wet hands. A 72px target, an ink stamp that lands slightly rotated with a short weighted thump, and the row settles into green. No confetti, no sound, no score.

Decisions taken, and what I need from you

Taken, from your answers
  • Canvas is 810 × 1080, portrait, fluid enough to survive a different iPad.
  • Projects is pointed — it gets the size ramp, the day counts stated bluntly, and no softening language. Everything else in the product is warm by contrast.
  • Holidays and Wants stay two modules. Slots and a decide-by on one, a queue with one active item on the other. They will cross-reference, not merge.
  • Chores ship with a rate line that is data, not layout — flat-weekly and per-job both render in the same row shape, so the family decision can land later without a redesign.
  • Avatars are flat initial discs in the four hues above, sized and positioned for illustration to drop in later.
Open — worth a minute before I build
  • The uniform answer on the dashboard. Your §13 asks it and I think yes — it and the week letter share the answer zone before school, which is the single biggest daily win in the product. It does cost the largest real estate on the wall to a question that is irrelevant from 9am.
  • Does the queued state show its blocker by name? "Waiting on the kitchen" is more useful than "Queued", but it is one step from the dependency graph you ruled out. I would show the name and stop there.
  • Two savings-won rows, one pending. The mortgage switch is worth ~$2,231/yr and is not done. Pending wins are motivating but a scoreboard of things that nearly happened is worse than a short honest one.
