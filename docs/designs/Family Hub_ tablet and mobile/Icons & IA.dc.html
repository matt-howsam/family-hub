01

Icons — Phosphor, Light weight

Phosphor Icons — MIT licence, free for commercial use, ~9,000 glyphs across six weights, available as a web font, plain SVG, or React package. It is the right library here for one specific reason: the Light weight draws at roughly a 1.5px stroke on a 24px grid, which is the same optical weight as the register's hairline rules. Most icon sets only offer a 2px stroke, which on this paper reads as heavy and app-like.

It also has the specific vocabulary this house needs and generic sets don't — broom, tent, hammer, piggy-bank, backpack, seal-check, sun-horizon. No compromise glyphs, no gear-icon-standing-in-for-a-lawnmower. One family, one weight, one stroke width, no mixing. Loaded here as the official web font from unpkg. Production would self-host a subset of the ~20 glyphs actually used, which is a few kilobytes and works offline — a fridge PWA cannot depend on a CDN.

Four rules, and the third is the important one
  • Ink or Mist only. Never brass, never green. An icon may not carry status. If a renewal needs attention, the type says so — an amber pictogram is the "wall of amber" failure mode arriving through the side door.
  • An icon never appears alone. Always beside its word, never as a naked target. A pictogram that has to be interpreted is slower than a word for four people of different ages, and unlike a word it can be got wrong.
  • Three legal positions. The module mark at the head of a stack row; the row-type mark inside a phone list; the verb mark inside a phone commit button. Nowhere else — and that means zero icons in the fridge answer zone and zero in the standing head. The wall stays print.
  • Two sizes. 28px on the wall, 20px on the phone. Both optically aligned to the cap-height of the label beside them, not to its baseline.

02

Illustration — Open Peeps, public domain

Open Peeps by Pablo Stanley — CC0, public domain, the freest licence there is: no attribution, no restrictions, nothing to renew. A hand-drawn library of heads, faces, expressions and hair that mix combinatorially, so four family members get four genuinely distinct characters rather than four palette swaps. Available as raw SVG (self-host, offline, zero dependency) or generated on the fly via the open-source DiceBear API, which wraps Open Peeps and several other CC0 sets.

Chosen over Big Heads — which the brief names, and which is also free (MIT) — for two reasons. Big Heads is a React component library, so the art is locked inside a runtime dependency rather than sitting in the project as files a fridge can cache. And its style is smooth, flat and vector-perfect, where Open Peeps is drawn with a visible pen — the same hand that filled in the timetable in blue biro. On this paper that matters more than it should.

Illustration appears in exactly two places

People, and the rare empty state. An avatar wherever a person is named or assigned. And a single Open Peeps figure, drawn small and off to one side, on the three or four screens that are genuinely empty — nothing stalled, nothing renewing, week not yet started. Those are the joy moments from the Design Plan and they are the only decorative art in the product.

No spot illustration on populated screens, no illustrated headers, no character mascot, and no illustrated empty state on a screen that is empty because something has gone wrong. A picture where a number should be is how a tool starts feeling like a toy — and Rose is thirteen, which is the whole audience risk in one sentence.

The object model — four nouns

Before screens, the things. Ten modules is a lot of product, and the only way it stays coherent is if they are all made of the same four nouns. Each noun has one decay rule and one row shape, so a new module is a query over existing types rather than a new design problem.

The fifth thing is Person, and it is deliberately not an object with a decay rule. Matt, Renée, Rose and Tom are attributes of the other four — a job is assigned to someone, an occasion involves someone, a number was logged by someone. There is no person record that accumulates a state, no per-child page that can be compared with a sibling's, and no way to sort the house by who is behind. That is principle 5 from the Design Plan enforced in the data model rather than in the layout, which is the only place it will actually hold.

04

Four containers, and no fifth

The entire product is built from four container types. Every screen in the map that follows is one of these. Adding a container type later is the thing most likely to wreck this, so they are named and numbered now.

C1
The PageFridge only · exactly one exists

The wall. Standing head, answer zone, row stack, avatar strip, footer. Read-only, always open, no back button because there is nowhere to go back to. It does not scroll and it never navigates away — a Page that has been left on a sub-screen is a wall that has stopped working, and that is the single worst failure mode in the product.

C2
The RowBoth · one module's answer in one line

A module's entire contribution to the wall: its mark, its label, one answer line, one qualifier. Not a card, not a tile, not a widget — a typeset line in a stack, hairline above and below. A module with nothing to say prints no Row at all.

C3
The SheetPhone only · a full module, scrollable

All eleven scorecard categories, all thirty-one register lines, all seven jobs. The place where the paper documents live in full. Reading and light editing; scrolls freely, has a title and a back.

C4
The SlipPhone only · one decision, one commit

Where every change to the house is made. One object, one field or one stage picker, one button ≥56px. Sensitive values — policy numbers, plates, the mortgage figure — exist only here. Dismissible, never nested, and it cannot open another Slip.

The rule that makes the whole thing navigable: depth never exceeds three. Sheet → Slip → commit, and you are back. There is no tab bar, no drawer, no settings tree. The phone's home is a single list of the ten Sheets, and a person is at most two taps from any change they are allowed to make.

What I need to build the next two

The register and the scorecard are in the project and I am working from them line by line. The brief itself is not — so I have the two paper documents but not the seven stalled jobs, their stages, or their day counts, and the Home Screen in State A is that list.

Drop the brief into the project and I will build both screens against the real seven — with day counts that are actually true, which is the difference between a mockup and a design. Inventing them would put the one thing you asked me to treat as source of truth into the one screen you are about to review.
